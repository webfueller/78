Field Note 001

August 2026

Founder's memo — not a survey

Five things to build, one to actually build.

Agents can act now. Nobody dares let them, because you cannot preview a consequence. That gap is the whole opportunity — and it is the last one on this list that a single person can still take.

The read

Three facts about August 2026, and what each one permits.

Duration

Capability stopped being the constraint; duration became it. Frontier agents finish work at fifty-percent reliability on a horizon measured in hours. On hard long-horizon benchmarks, the state of the art sits under twenty percent. The published failure modes are not intelligence failures — they are bookkeeping failures. Subplan drift. Forgetting a constraint that surfaced mid-task. Declaring done. Never verifying. That is a missing-environment problem, and environments are buildable by one person.

The rails

The infrastructure land grab is over before most people noticed it started. Agent identity, agent payments, agent authorization — ACP, UCP, AP2, A2A, TAP — are being standardized this year by companies with balance sheets and distribution. Do not open a toll booth next to Stripe's toll booth.

Distribution

The products that actually broke out were single-purpose, and their output was the advertisement. Nobody watched a demo. They saw a friend's artifact and wanted one of their own. Design the artifact first; the product is what has to exist for the artifact to be real.

The filter

Five gates. Fail one and it is not a one-person company.

The artifact test

What it produces has to be worth posting. If growth needs a referral program, it is already dead.

The escape-velocity test

It improves with use in a way nobody can copy off the homepage. Prompts are not a moat. Accumulated environment state is.

The zero-ops test

No sales calls, no onboarding, no support queue that grows with revenue. It sells, teaches, and debugs itself.

The frontier test

Barely possible now, comfortable in eighteen months. Too easy means forty clones by Christmas. Too hard means you are a research lab with no revenue.

The consequence test

It touches money, a deadline, or a relationship. Toys spike and die, and the spike is indistinguishable from the real thing for about six weeks.

Rejected without further thought

Agent app stores — no defensibility. Agent identity and payment rails — see above. Another coding agent — a capital-intensive knife fight. Companions — retention rented from a model provider. Anything phrased "ChatGPT for X" where X is a feature.

The five

Each one attacks a specific place where agents break.

01

Logbook — the agent that does not forget

An agent that owns one goal for a year. The innovation is not the agent; it is the world file — a single human-readable dossier of your situation that the agent rewrites every day and re-reads before acting. Wake, read, take one to five real actions, write back. The dossier is the fix for drift, and it is also the product: your own life, legible, serialized.

Breaks
Catastrophic forgetting, state drift, premature completion.
Artifact
The dossier. "Day 214 of my agent getting me out of debt."
Money
Subscription priced against the outcome, never the tokens.
Moat
The world file. Switching cost compounds daily and silently.
How it dies
The gap between day one and day sixty. Nothing visible happens in week two.
02The pick

Antechamber — rehearse the week before you live it

Your accounts, forked into a shadow copy. An agent runs a week of real work inside the fork — including replies from the people involved, modeled on your actual history with each of them. You get a branch map of futures. You commit one. Only then does anything touch the world, with a receipt and a hard undo window behind it.

Breaks
Planning and subplanning failure, irreversibility, unverified claims.
Artifact
The branch map. And: "my agent simulated my landlord, and he said no in every timeline."
Money
Consumer subscription, then per-seat anywhere outbound work carries stakes.
Moat
Twin fidelity plus per-relationship counterparty models. Unclonable from outside the account.
How it dies
The simulated people are unconvincing, and it becomes an expensive toy.
03

The Pact — agents with teeth

Not a habit tracker. You sign a mandate with real stakes — money, a disclosure, a message that sends itself — and an agent with genuine account access enforces it. The novel technical problem is that the principal is the adversary: the user spends the month trying to jailbreak their own agent, and the agent has to hold.

Breaks
Irreversible action under an adversarial principal; authority that must survive its owner's second thoughts.
Artifact
Consequences. Enforcement is inherently content.
Money
A cut of the stake, plus float on escrow.
Moat
Trust and a public track record — which is also brutal to bootstrap.
How it dies
One bad enforcement, one press cycle. The regulatory edge is live from day one.
04

Stamp — the adversarial verifier

Agents claim; nobody checks. Build the checker — an independent adversary that takes an agent's claimed work, hunts for evidence it actually happened, and issues a verdict. Consumer skin: paste any claim, get a stamp.

Breaks
The most-cited failure dimension of the year: verification and reflection.
Artifact
The verdict. Stamps become a format people quote at each other.
Money
Per-verification, then embedded inside other people's products.
Moat
The corpus of what was verified, how, and what turned out to be false.
How it dies
Negative-sum posture, thin willingness to pay, and every agent vendor ships a free self-check.
05

The Commons — a world other people's agents live in

A persistent world whose inhabitants are agents belonging to real people, with real scarcity, real reputation, and real accumulated history. You write your agent's charter; it lives there around the clock and sends you dispatches.

Breaks
Multi-agent adversarial play, long horizons, emergent negotiation under incomplete information.
Artifact
The dispatches. Short-form native, and they write themselves.
Money
Subscription plus scarcity.
Moat
The world itself. Nobody clones three years of lore and two hundred thousand inhabitants.
How it dies
Retention, and inference cost per idle inhabitant. It is a game, and games are hit-driven.

The pick

Antechamber. Sell the ground every agent stands on.

Every other concept here is downstream of the same fact: agents can act, and nobody dares let them, because a consequence cannot be previewed. Solve preview and you are not selling an agent — you are selling the ground the agents stand on. That is the only position on this list where the hard part compounds into something a competitor cannot read off your homepage.

It passes the frontier test precisely. Simulating a plausible week of your working life, with counterparties who behave like the actual humans, is barely achievable right now. In eighteen months it is table stakes — which is exactly the window one person wants.

The v1 wedge — one domain, cheap to mirror, high stakes, high frequency

  1. Connect mail and calendar, read-only. Fork a shadow copy as an event-sourced store with full replay.
  2. Give the agent a mandate. Clear the backlog. Reschedule what's slipping. Cancel what I don't use.
  3. It runs seven simulated days inside the fork. Counterparties reply, modeled on your real history with each of them.
  4. You get a branch map: committed path, simulated paths, dead paths.
  5. You commit one branch. It executes for real, produces a receipt, and stays undoable for twenty-four hours.

The counterparty models are the whole company. They are the hard part, the moat, and the thing people screenshot.

Launch mechanic — no account, no OAuth, no friction

Paste the hardest email you have to send this week. Get a branch map of how it goes. Free, no sign-up, counterparty inferred from the pasted thread alone. The account comes later, when someone wants the fork of their real week.

Kill criteria — write these down before the first commit

  • Counterparty backtest does not beat a trivial baseline by week four → kill it. Without that number the twin is theatre and you are selling a mood.
  • Fewer than one in four people who see a branch map make a second one within seven days → the artifact is not an artifact. Fix it or stop.
  • Inference cost per rehearsal exceeds monthly price divided by expected runs, with no line of sight to closing the gap → the unit economics never arrive.

The live wire

Simulating real, named people is this product's power and its exposure. Two rules from day one, not retrofitted after the first complaint. Model response classes and likelihoods, not a named person's voice as quotable output. And never let simulated content leave the owner's private preview in a form that could be mistaken for something a real person actually said. Get this wrong once and the story about the company writes itself.

The honest bear case

Twin fidelity may plateau below the level where anyone changes a decision because of it. If the branch map is merely interesting rather than decisive, this is a beautiful demo with a three-week retention curve. Week four's backtest is the entire bet. Run it early, and believe the number.
